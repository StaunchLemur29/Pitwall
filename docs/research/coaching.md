# How driver coaching works, and what learning science says

Research notes for Pitwall. Gathered November 2026.

**About the sources.** This session's network blocked direct page fetches, so most papers were checked through their abstracts, index records and the summaries other papers give of them, not the full text. Treat exact effect sizes as "roughly this". The links go to the record or abstract.

## The short version

- Coaches teach a few basics first (where you look, braking, the line), then one thing at a time.
- Feedback helps most when it comes **after** the lap, not during it. It should be short, often a summary over several laps, and given only when the error is bigger than a set tolerance.
- Varied practice feels worse but sticks better than doing the same thing over and over.
- Several "famous" effects in motor learning are smaller than once thought: focus of attention, self-chosen feedback, confidence boosts. They are still cheap to apply, so don't build big features on them.
- For an ADHD adult: one cue at a time, quick visible wins, structure the app holds for you, and no nagging.

## What coaches focus on, and in what order

**Vision comes first.** Ross Bentley (Speed Secrets) teaches drivers to look far ahead and turn their head, and to look at where braking should *end* rather than where it starts: "if you look into where you want to end your braking ... you'll find that where you begin braking is easier to find" ([Bentley, Substack](https://rossbentley.substack.com/p/speed-secrets-just-5-seconds); [glance and focus](https://rossbentley.substack.com/p/speed-secrets-glance-and-focus-your)). Lab work agrees. A Formula 3 driver's head direction predicted the car's rotation about one second later ([Land & Tatler 2001](https://discovery.dundee.ac.uk/en/publications/steering-with-the-head-the-visual-strategy-of-a-racing-driver/)). In a simulator, racing drivers looked further ahead and turned their heads more than non-racers, while their reaction times were no better ([van Leeuwen et al. 2017, PLoS ONE](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29121090/)). The gap was technique, not reflexes.

**Then braking and the line.** At racing schools, braking comes early. A Skip Barber school classroom session covers footwork, weight transfer, braking and where to place the car ([Digital Trends](https://digitaltrends.com/cars/lucas-oil-school-of-racing)). One student's account of the three-day course has day one opening with threshold braking and the line, then a threshold braking exercise ([Rennlist](https://rennlist.com/forums/997-gt2-gt3-forum/377327-skip-barber-3-day-school.html)). Track-day teaching builds every corner on five reference points: braking point, turn-in, slowest point, apex and exit ([NASA Speed News](https://nasaspeed.news/columns/driver-instruction/track-walks-the-what-where-why-and-how/)).

**Consistency, and one thing at a time.** Bentley's programmes list speed, consistency and confidence side by side ([event listing](https://www.motorsportreg.com/events/parktown-imports-ross-bentley-s-speed-secrets-st-louis-frontenac-hilton-pca-139289)). He sometimes runs a whole session on a single sense (only sight, only feel, or only sound) to sharpen what the driver notices ([Winding Road, be a more sensitive driver](https://windingroad.com/articles/features/speed-secrets-be-a-more-sensitive-driver/)). One coaching course summary says to pick "one primary process goal and, at most, one support cue" ([PaddockLink, secondary source](https://paddocklink-docs.pages.dev/lms/coaching-science/sustain-motivation-and-self-regulation/reduce-controlling-pressure/)).

## How sessions are structured

The common pattern across these schools is a short briefing, one focus for the session, laps, then a review. Bentley's masterclass covers "how to practice and self-coach", then on-track drills ([Rocky Mountain Motorsports](https://rockymotorsports.com/calendar/experience-rmm/speed-secrets-on-track-masterclass/)). Sim coaches such as Coach Dave compare a lap with a reference, split it into sections and go after the biggest gap first ([Coach Dave Academy](https://coachdaveacademy.com/tutorials/learn-a-new-track-faster-using-coach-dave-delta/)).

## What the learning science says

**Deliberate practice.** Focused practice on a specific weakness, with feedback and repetition, is what separates experts from people who simply log hours. Years of experience on their own predict performance weakly ([Ericsson 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18778378/); [Ericsson et al. 1993](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.02396/full)). Practice still isn't everything: in sport it explained about 18% of the differences in performance ([Macnamara et al. 2014](https://stafforini.com/works/macnamara-2014-deliberate-practice-and/); [2016 sport-only meta-analysis](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1745691616635600)). For someone with 600 hours, *how* they practise now matters more than how much.

**Blocked vs varied practice.** Repeating one corner over and over (blocked practice) looks better during the session. Mixing things up (random or varied practice) gives better retention and transfer. A 2024 meta-analysis of 34 studies found a medium benefit for transfer (about 0.55) in adults ([Czyż et al. 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39205981/)). An earlier meta-analysis found blocked practice ahead during practice but no difference at retention ([Jiménez-Díaz et al.](https://kimuk.conare.ac.cr/Record/PUCR_fe7511d610b969b3d1d9a7a33ac0b878)).

**Feedback frequency and the guidance hypothesis.** Feedback after every attempt can make learners lean on it and stop reading their own feel ([guidance hypothesis, summarised in Wulf et al. 2010](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2010.00190/full)). The picture is mixed. Frequent feedback seems fine when it points at an external effect, and complex skills don't always follow the simple-task rules ([Wulf et al. 2010](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2010.00190/full); [Wulf & Shea 2002](https://www.doi.org/10.3758/BF03196276)). Driving is a complex skill, so treat "less is more" as a sensible default, not a law.

**Live feedback during a lap.** Continuous feedback during a movement makes practice look better but hurts what you can do once it's gone ([Schmidt & Wulf 1997, Human Factors](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1518%2F001872097778667979)). The authors drew the lesson specifically for simulators. Feedback given *instantly* after an attempt also hurts, because it stops you judging the attempt yourself first. Learners who estimate their own error before seeing the result learn better ([Swinnen et al. 1990, summarised here](https://raw.github.com/cartermaclab/carterlab-papers/main/2019_barros-etal_examining-the-impact-of-error-estimation-on-the-effects-of-self-controlled-feedback.pdf)).

**Bandwidth feedback.** Only give feedback when the error falls outside a tolerance band, and say nothing (meaning "good") inside it. This beat simply giving less feedback, and it made performance more consistent at retention ([Lee & Carnahan 1990](https://experts.mcmaster.ca/scholarly-works/1313886)).

**Summary feedback.** Feedback that sums up several attempts hurt practice performance but helped retention ([Schmidt et al. 1989](https://lirias.kuleuven.be/836415)). Part of that benefit may just come from giving feedback less often ([discussion](https://scilit.com/publications/28069957a3181cae0a6bcf2d57974a69)).

**Feedback after good attempts.** Learners who heard about their best attempts learned more than those who heard about their worst, even though both groups practised equally well ([Chiviacowsky & Wulf 2007](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/abs/10.1080/02701367.2007.10599402)).

**Focus of attention.** Cues about the *effect* ("carry speed to the kerb") beat cues about the *body* ("ease your right foot"). A 2021 meta-analysis found benefits for retention and transfer ([Chua et al. 2021](https://digitalscholarship.unlv.edu/kns_fac_articles/424); [Wulf 2013 review](https://scinapse.io/papers/2044201858)). A newer re-analysis argues publication bias explains much of this, so the effect may be small ([McKay et al. preprint](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/304)). Wording cues externally costs nothing, so do it anyway.

**Self-controlled feedback and confidence.** Letting learners choose when they get feedback, and boosting their confidence, form two of the three pillars of OPTIMAL theory ([Wulf & Lewthwaite 2016](https://oasis.library.unlv.edu/kns_fac_articles/123)). After correcting for bias, both effects shrink to roughly zero ([McKay & Ste-Marie 2022](https://raw.github.com/cartermaclab/carterlab-papers/main/2022_mckay-etal_meta-analytic-findings-of-the-self-controlled-motor-learning-literature-underpowered-biased-and-lacking-evidential-value.pdf); [McKay et al. 2025](https://experts.mcmaster.ca/scholarly-works/3159192)). Offering a choice is still good for motivation and costs nothing. Just don't expect it to speed up learning.

**Spacing.** Spreading practice out beats cramming it, with moderate to large effects in older meta-analyses ([Donovan & Radosevich 1999](https://www.gwern.net/docs/spaced-repetition/1999-donovan.pdf); [Lee & Genovese 1988](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/abs/10.1080/02701367.1988.10609373)). The evidence is thinner for complex skills, and one 2025 trial favoured short gaps between attempts ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40778584/)). In practice: several shorter sessions on a weak section across days, rather than one marathon.

## What suits an ADHD adult self-learner

- **Working memory is tighter.** A meta-analysis of 38 studies found moderate working-memory deficits in adults with ADHD ([Alderson et al. 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23688211/)). Carrying one cue into a lap is realistic. Carrying three isn't.
- **Waiting is costly.** Adults with ADHD prefer small, quick rewards over larger, later ones ([delay-aversion research](https://link.springer.com/10.1038/s41598-018-24944-5)). Short loops with a clear "done" moment help.
- **Interest drives hyperfocus.** Hyperfocus starts with interest and is hard to stop. It helps when the setting is flexible and hurts in loose routines. External structure such as reminders and planned breaks helps ([European Psychiatry 2025](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/european-psychiatry/article/hyperfocus-in-adhd-a-misunderstood-cognitive-phenomenon/1DE44A25A69E8CE17FBC720D26AC2230); [Hupfeld et al. 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30267329/)).
- Several of these research findings point the same way: one focus at a time, feedback on success, little live chatter. They suit ADHD needs too.

## What this means for Pitwall

Ranked, most useful first.

1. **One focus per session, carried by the app.** Pick the single biggest gain (for example "brake release into Corner X") and make it the only live cue. Everything else waits for the debrief. *Partly exists:* time to beat, Good and bad review. *New:* an explicit "today's one thing" that the live card, drills and debrief all share.
2. **Make live cues quieter over time.** Keep the brake countdown for new corners, then fade it, for example after a few clean laps inside tolerance, so the driver learns their own markers. This follows Schmidt & Wulf 1997 and the guidance hypothesis. *Exists:* brake cues from the optimal run. *New:* the fading rule.
3. **Bandwidth feedback.** Stay silent while a corner is within tolerance of its time to beat, and flag it only when it falls outside. This cuts clutter and matches Lee & Carnahan. *New.*
4. **Guess first, then see.** Before the review, ask one tap: "Which corner felt worst?" Then show the data. This is Swinnen's error estimation, cheap to build and it trains self-coaching. *New.*
5. **Lead with what went right.** Debriefs open with the best corner or section of the session, then the one fix. This follows Chiviacowsky & Wulf and avoids nagging. *Partly exists* (Good and bad review). Check the order and keep "bad" to one item.
6. **Summary feedback over a stint.** Review after 3–5 laps or at the end of a drill, not after every lap. *Exists* in the AI debrief. Consider a short mid-session summary too.
7. **Mix up drills.** Once a section is roughly learnt, rotate two or three sections, or vary the start (different entry speeds) instead of repeating one. *Exists:* drills with retry and teleport. *New:* a "mixed" mode.
8. **Word cues as effects, not body parts.** Write "carry speed to the exit kerb", not "less brake pressure". Applies to the live card and the Claude debrief prompt. *Prompt change.*
9. **Spread practice across days.** The hub could suggest coming back to a weak section in the next session rather than grinding it now. Suggest, don't nag. *New, low priority.*
10. **Optional choice.** Let the driver ask for detail when they want it ("show me why") rather than pushing it. It's fine for motivation, but don't expect learning gains (McKay 2022). *Partly exists.*
